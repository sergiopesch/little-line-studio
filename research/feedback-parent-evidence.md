# Feedback, parent participation, and bounded voice instruction

Research checked 25 October 2026. These are primary empirical sources, not endorsements of a commercial teaching method. The transfer from human feedback, reading, and video chat to an AI drawing app is an inference unless stated otherwise. No source establishes that this complete product is proven effective or clinically beneficial.

## 1. Parent scaffolding and drawing: direct, but observational

**Straffon et al. (2024), Developing drawing skill.** [Published paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2024.101444); [opened university-hosted full text](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/dc5cd26b-ed0d-43cb-b15d-38adddd6981b/content).

- **Sample/design:** 68 parent–child dyads, child ages 3.0–6.9; cross-sectional parent questionnaire and Beery visual–motor integration assessment.
- **Result:** adding four parent-support constructs did not significantly improve the overall model, ΔR²=.08, p=.207. The scaffolding component was positively associated with scores, b=1.02, p=.022; praise, resources, and joint drawing were not significant individual predictors.
- **Limits:** self-reported support, restricted demographic range, 91% highly educated parents; observational association cannot establish causation. VMI figure copying is narrower than creativity or general artistic quality. Component findings in a null overall block warrant replication.
- **Product inference:** make the parent an active source of hints and demonstrations. Do not advertise that parent presence alone improves drawing, nor that additional praise does so.

## 2. Process versus person feedback in kindergarten

**Kamins & Dweck (1999), Person Versus Process Praise and Criticism.** [Opened full text](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/infancy/pdf/person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf).

- **Sample/design:** two experiments: 67 kindergarteners (mean 5 years 7 months) receiving criticism and 64 (mean 5 years 6 months) receiving praise. Children role-played teacher feedback and subsequent setbacks with dolls and props.
- **Result:** person-focused feedback produced less adaptive responses than process feedback. In the praise experiment, persistence averaged 0.97 versus 1.53 on a 0–2 index, p<.01.
- **Limits:** immediate role-play measures in small school samples; no lasting drawing-learning outcome. Several feedback variants were combined into categories.
- **Product inference:** describe strategies and actions; avoid making success a judgment of the child's identity or worth. This does not justify mechanically praising effort on every turn or claiming long-term resilience gains.

## 3. Ability versus effort praise in older children

**Mueller & Dweck (1998), Praise for Intelligence Can Undermine Children's Motivation and Performance.** [Opened Columbia-hosted full text](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf).

- **Design:** six experiments with fifth graders using nonverbal reasoning problems. As one concrete example, Study 4 included 51 children aged 9–11 (mean 9.9), assigned to intelligence, effort, or control feedback.
- **Result:** in Study 4, 76% of intelligence-praised children chose peer-performance information rather than strategy information, versus 24% of effort-praised children. Intelligence-praised children endorsed more fixed beliefs than effort-praised children. Across the paper, post-setback persistence, enjoyment, and performance often favoured effort praise.
- **Limits:** short-term academic tasks, much of the age range at or above our upper bound; not a drawing intervention. Study 4's goal-choice difference was nonsignificant, so the evidence is not uniformly positive across measures.
- **Product inference:** avoid labels such as “natural artist” and peer ranking. Offer useful strategies rather than a blanket “try harder.”

## 4. Early parent praise and later motivation

**Gunderson et al. (2013), Parent Praise to 1–3-Year-Olds Predicts Children's Motivational Frameworks 5 Years Later.** [Opened full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123/).

- **Sample/design:** 53 Chicago-area families; naturalistic 90-minute home recordings at 14, 26, and 38 months; motivational questionnaires at ages 7–8.
- **Result:** proportion of process praise predicted later incremental motivational frameworks, r=.35, p=.01. Total praise did not explain this association; predicted negative association with person praise was not found (r=−.05, p=.73).
- **Limits:** longitudinal correlation, small sample, unmeasured child/family differences and intervening parenting remain possible explanations. Outcomes were motivational beliefs rather than drawing skill.
- **Product inference:** use accurate process-related feedback and teach parents examples. Do not claim that a particular AI phrase causes a durable mindset or that all person praise is demonstrably harmful in every context.

## 5. Inflated praise after a drawing

**Brummelman et al. (2014), “That's Not Just Beautiful—That's Incredibly Beautiful!”** [Opened University of Amsterdam full text](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/18760261/That_s_Not_Just_Beautiful_That_s_Incredibly_Beautiful.pdf).

- **Sample/design:** Study 3 randomized 240 Dutch museum visitors aged 8–12 (mean 9.85) to inflated, ordinary, or no praise from an ostensible professional painter after drawing.
- **Result:** inflated praise reduced subsequent challenge seeking among children with lower measured self-esteem, but increased it among those with higher self-esteem. The result is an interaction, not a universal negative effect.
- **Limits:** one phrase, short-term challenge choice, largely Dutch sample; mechanism was not directly measured. Do not extrapolate the effect to all preschoolers.
- **Product inference:** choose warm, proportionate acknowledgements over habitual superlatives. Do not profile children's self-esteem or infer psychological traits from their drawing to select praise.

## 6. Screens can crowd out the parent

**Munzer et al. (2019), Differences in Parent-Toddler Interactions With Electronic Versus Print Books.** [Primary article](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2012); [opened full-text copy](https://www.babyoralhealthprogram.org/uploads/files/Electronic%20Versus%20Print%20Books.pdf).

- **Sample/design:** 37 dyads, toddlers mean 29.2 months; counterbalanced within-dyad comparison of enhanced electronic, basic electronic, and print books, five minutes per condition.
- **Result:** parent dialogic-talk intervals: print 11.9, enhanced 6.2, basic 8.3 (both electronic comparisons p<.001). Collaboration also favoured print. Shared positive affect was similar.
- **Limits:** toddlers below our target age, one commercial book application, laboratory context, mostly educated parents. Learning itself was not assessed.
- **Product inference:** supervision should mean co-participation, supported by a shared view and minimal interface management. Test whether the voice/interface suppress parent–child conversation. This study does not show that all digital co-use is inferior.

## 7. Contingency matters more than merely playing a voice

**Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff (2014), Skype Me!** [Opened publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cdev.12166).

- **Sample/design:** 36 toddlers aged 24–30 months, randomly assigned to live, contingent video-chat, or yoked prerecorded-video novel-verb instruction (12 per condition).
- **Result:** evidence of learning occurred in the two socially contingent conditions, not the prerecorded condition.
- **Limits:** very small groups, toddlers below target ages, short-term language learning, human interlocutors. This is not evidence that generative voice AI improves drawing or that prerecorded drawing demonstrations are ineffective.
- **Product inference:** responsive help may add value beyond narration, but should address the child's actual request. It does not require constant listening or interruption.

## 8. Scripted conversational agents can support a bounded learning activity

**Xu et al. (2022), Dialogue with a conversational agent promotes children's story comprehension via enhancing engagement.** [Opened university-hosted full text](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1rw6t612/qt1rw6t612.pdf).

- **Sample/design:** 117 children aged 3–6 randomized in a 2×2 design: dialogic/non-dialogic reading × agent/human; approximately 15-minute session, immediate comprehension test.
- **Result:** dialogic reading had a 0.51 SD adjusted association with comprehension versus non-dialogic conditions, p<.001. Reading-partner main effect and interaction were nonsignificant; that is not a formal proof of equivalence. Raw comprehension means: agent dialogic 11.6, agent non-dialogic 8.67, human dialogic 11.2, human non-dialogic 9.88.
- **Limits:** scripted questions/feedback, affluent/high-language-proficiency sample, one short intervention, immediate story outcome; mediation analyses used data-dependent covariate selection.
- **Product inference:** tightly bounded voice turns are plausible. This does not validate an unrestricted LLM, long-term benefits, parent replacement, or drawing instruction efficacy.

## Proposed teaching rules to test (inferences, not proven product effects)

1. Give a specific, short instruction or demonstration, then yield the floor.
2. Let explicit controls request repetition, hints, and progression; never infer readiness or distress from silence.
3. On help, offer an attention cue, then a demonstration, then an easier variation. Let parents decide whether more support is wanted.
4. Use factual observations only when perceptually supported. When unsure, ask the child to describe their intention. Do not fabricate evidence of effort or improvement.
5. Use occasional specific encouragement, with no talent labels, peer comparison, grades, or exaggerated praise.
6. Preserve parent–child dialogue and child agency. Invite explanation at natural boundaries, without turning every mark into a quiz.
7. Separate outcomes: enjoyment, agency, instruction comprehension, retention, and transfer to an unfamiliar subject/on paper. Completing a copy or staying longer is not sufficient evidence of learning.
8. The reviewed sources do not establish an optimal silence length, speaking rate, number of words per instruction, or frequency of feedback for this product. Those parameters require age-stratified usability and learning studies, including accessibility variation.
