# Expert drawing: authored curriculum proposal

26 October 2026. Content design only; these sequences have not been reviewed by an art educator or tested with families. **Expert** names a set of more complex drawing challenges. It is not a child label, certification, prerequisite, or claim of mastery. Families choose by interest, comfort, and access needs rather than a rigid age band.

## Teaching contract

The child draws on real paper alongside a supervising parent. The website demonstrates on its own read-only board. It has no view of the paper and must not comment as though it has seen the child's marks, effort, feelings, or progress. Parent acknowledgement is not verification of continuous presence.

Each lesson has 20 individually numbered drawing actions. Each numbered action is one proposed teaching turn; its hint is available through **Help**, not spoken automatically. Show the current demonstration slowly, leave its still image available, then wait silently for as long as the family wants. **Next** alone advances; **Repeat** replays the current action. Silence and completion of playback never advance. The family may revisit, skip a detail, pause, or finish at any point without a penalty. Optional marks are invitations, not completion requirements.

Preparation and observation happen before the numbered drawing actions; exploration and later transfer are separate invitations afterwards. Each main sequence therefore has 20 drawing turns plus three short non-drawing stages: preparation, observation, and exploration. Later transfer is a different-day activity, not an extra compulsory turn. A drawing action can make a single shape or continuous contour; it need not demand a perfectly unbroken pencil stroke. Avoid voice interruptions during a mark. Written instructions remain usable without speech; microphone capture is not needed for these lessons.

Use the demonstration as one possible arrangement. Different proportions, gaps, wobbles, omissions, and imaginative departures are welcome. Do not require erasing or matching an outline. A parent can point, describe one relationship, or demonstrate on separate paper when asked; the child's marks remain their own. An alternative accessible movement can replace any action. Allow a larger picture or fewer details without assigning a lower level.

- [Teaching methodology review](../teaching-methodology-review.md), especially sections 1–5 and 7: combine noticing, demonstration, optional scaffolding, and family-controlled pauses. The review does not establish the exact sequences, word counts, or turn counts below.
- [Drawing instruction evidence](../drawing-instruction-evidence.md): observation coaching has small direct experimental support; cube studies caution that copying a prescribed order alone does not establish transfer. Each lesson therefore identifies a relationship to notice and a later new subject.
- [Feedback and parent evidence](../feedback-parent-evidence.md): parent scaffolding and strategy-focused language inform the proposed support. Observational associations are not proof that adult presence or these parent prompts cause learning.
- [Pacing and practice evidence](../pacing-practice-medium-evidence.md): learner control and later revisiting are plausible adaptations from narrower tasks, not a validated drawing schedule. These lessons make no scientific efficacy promise.

Review understanding, agency, enjoyment, parent workload, and later independent attempts separately; neither a completed copy nor a longer session proves learning. There are no scores, timers, inferred readiness, or automatic assessments.

## 1. Bird on a branch

**Objective:** arrange joined body parts around a perch and explore which parts overlap.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose a spacious part of the paper with room below the bird for a branch.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at a bird photograph, picture book, or the authored reference together. Notice where the feet meet the branch and how the wing sits inside the body. A picture is enough; observing a live bird is optional.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one sloping line for the branch. | Leave room above it for your bird. |
| 2. Draw one line below it for the branch's lower edge. | Follow the first line loosely. |
| 3. Join the branch edges at the left end with one curve. | A rounded end is enough. |
| 4. Join the branch edges at the right end with one curve. | The ends can be different. |
| 5. Draw one oval above the branch for the body. | Leave a little space for legs. |
| 6. Draw one smaller circle touching the body's upper right for the head. | Let the shapes overlap if you like. |
| 7. Draw one pointed beak on the head's right side. | A little triangle can work. |
| 8. Add one dot inside the head for an eye. | Choose a spot behind the beak. |
| 9. Draw one curved wing shape inside the body. | A sideways teardrop is one idea. |
| 10. Draw one long tail shape from the body's lower left. | A narrow leaf shape can work. |
| 11. Draw one leg from the body down to the branch. | A short line is enough. |
| 12. Draw one toe along the branch from that leg. | Try a small sideways line. |
| 13. Draw one second leg down to the branch. | It can start beside the first. |
| 14. Draw one toe along the branch from the second leg. | The toes can point towards the beak. |
| 15. Add one short line inside the wing. **Optional.** | This can suggest a feather. |
| 16. Add one short line inside the tail. **Optional.** | Follow the tail's direction. |
| 17. Draw one twig from the branch. **Optional.** | Choose a gap away from the feet. |
| 18. If you added a twig, draw one leaf on it. **Optional.** | A pointed oval can work. Without a twig, you can leave this out. |
| 19. Draw one cloud above the bird. **Optional.** | Leave some sky between them. |
| 20. Add one small patch on the bird's chest. **Optional.** | Choose its shape yourself. |

**Parent scaffold:** if requested, point to the reference's body, wing, and perch in turn. Ask, “Which part sits inside another?” Demonstrate a perch on separate paper rather than repositioning the child's feet.

**Simplification:** one branch line, an oval, head, and beak can communicate the idea. Feet may be single lines; skip the wing interior and all optional details.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** choose whether this bird is visiting, singing, sheltering, or doing something entirely different. Invent a feature or setting that changes its story.

**Later transfer:** another day, try a bird on a fence from a different reference, without replaying the original steps. Look again at the contact between feet and perch; copying the original bird is unnecessary.

## 2. Sleeping fox

**Objective:** use a large enclosing curve and smaller tucked shapes to suggest a curled pose.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose a broad space for the curled body and tail.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at a photograph or drawing of a curled fox. Notice how the tail can lie in front of the body and how little of the legs is visible. This lesson uses a stylised side view; it is not an anatomy test.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one wide oval for the fox's curled body. | A low, broad shape gives room to curl. |
| 2. Draw one small rounded head shape at the oval's right end. | It can overlap the body. |
| 3. Draw one pointed muzzle from the head towards the left. | Think of a short rounded wedge. |
| 4. Add one small nose at the muzzle's tip. | A dot is enough. |
| 5. Draw one pointed ear above the head. | A soft triangle can work. |
| 6. Draw one smaller ear just behind it. | Part of it can be hidden. |
| 7. Draw one closed-eye curve on the head. | A little downward bend suggests rest. |
| 8. Draw one curved cheek line inside the head. | Let it lead towards the muzzle. |
| 9. Draw one tucked paw shape below the muzzle. | A small oval can sit near the chin. |
| 10. Draw one long tail curve from the body's left side towards the paw. | Sweep around the body's lower edge. |
| 11. Draw one returning curve to give the tail its width. | Leave a broad middle and a narrow tip. |
| 12. Add one curved boundary across the tail's tip. | This marks a possible pale tip. |
| 13. Draw one curved hind-leg line inside the body. | A short arc can suggest a tucked leg. |
| 14. Add one short toe line on the paw. **Optional.** | A tiny mark is plenty. |
| 15. Draw one small inner-ear shape. **Optional.** | Keep it inside the larger ear. |
| 16. Add one short fur mark on the cheek. **Optional.** | Choose a place near the outer edge. |
| 17. Draw one shallow ground curve below the fox. **Optional.** | Leave room around the curled shape. |
| 18. Draw one leaf beside the fox. **Optional.** | Let it rest somewhere of your choosing. |
| 19. Draw one small stone near the ground curve. **Optional.** | A rounded shape can work. |
| 20. Add one short fur mark on the tail. **Optional.** | Follow the direction of the tail. |

**Parent scaffold:** use a finger above the reference to trace the tail's route without touching the child's work. If asked, show a simple curl on separate paper. Ask which parts the child wants visible.

**Simplification:** make the fox from a body oval, head, one ear, closed eye, and one broad tail shape. Visible construction overlaps can stay; there is no requirement to erase them.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** decide where the fox rests and what makes that place comfortable. A blanket, snowy hollow, imaginary den, or different setting changes the picture meaningfully.

**Later transfer:** try a curled cat or dog on another day. Look for the big outer curve and choose which limbs to hide, using a new reference if helpful.

## 3. Side-view elephant

**Objective:** compare large and small parts and represent near and partly hidden legs.

**Preparation — no drawing:** leave room at one side of the paper for a trunk. The proposed elephant faces right; the family may mirror the whole arrangement.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at an elephant side view. Notice the head's size beside the body, where the trunk begins, and which legs overlap. This drawing uses simplified shapes rather than exact anatomy.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one large oval for the body. | Make room underneath for legs. |
| 2. Draw one smaller rounded head at the body's right end. | Let it touch or overlap the body. |
| 3. Draw one large ear shape inside the head's left side. | A broad curved leaf is one idea. |
| 4. Draw one long trunk curve down from the face. | Turn the tip slightly if you like. |
| 5. Draw one returning curve beside it. | Give the trunk space between its edges. |
| 6. Join the trunk edges with one small curve at the tip. | A rounded end is enough. |
| 7. Add one eye above the trunk. | A little dot will do. |
| 8. Draw one near front-leg shape below the body. | A long rounded rectangle can work. |
| 9. Draw one near back-leg shape below the body's left side. | Leave a gap between the legs. |
| 10. Draw one partly hidden front-leg shape beside the near leg. | Let it start behind the first shape. |
| 11. Draw one partly hidden back-leg shape beside the near back leg. | A narrow visible part is enough. |
| 12. Draw one tail curve from the body's left end. | Let it hang or swing. |
| 13. Add one small tuft shape at the tail's end. | A little leaf shape can work. |
| 14. Draw one small tusk curve beside the trunk. **Optional.** | A short pointed crescent is one option. |
| 15. Add one curved crease across the trunk. **Optional.** | Keep it between the two edges. |
| 16. Add one small toenail curve on a foot. **Optional.** | One toenail is enough. |
| 17. Add one second toenail curve on that foot. **Optional.** | Leave a little space beside the first. |
| 18. Draw one ground line below the feet. **Optional.** | It can be gently uneven. |
| 19. Draw one grass blade near the ground. **Optional.** | A short bent line is enough. |
| 20. Draw one puddle shape nearby. **Optional.** | A flat oval is one possibility. |

**Parent scaffold:** compare the body and head in the reference without prescribing a ratio. Use separate paper to show how a narrow leg can appear from behind another. Help choose a larger trunk movement if small curves are awkward.

**Simplification:** use two visible legs, one trunk line, and no tusk or toenails. A full four-leg arrangement is an optional challenge, not a correctness requirement.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** choose an elephant activity or destination. Change the trunk pose, surroundings, or a companion to support that story.

**Later transfer:** draw a rhinoceros or another large animal from the side on a different day. Look at body-to-head size and overlapping legs rather than reusing the elephant's parts unchanged.

## 4. Rabbit looking sideways

**Objective:** arrange a side-facing head, differently placed ears, and a large tucked hind leg.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose a space with room above the head for long ears.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at a side-facing rabbit. Notice that one eye is usually prominent and the hind leg can make a large rounded shape beside the body. Decide which way this rabbit faces.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one broad oval for the body. | Leave space on the right for a head. |
| 2. Draw one smaller oval at the body's upper right for the head. | The ovals can touch. |
| 3. Draw one rounded muzzle shape at the head's right edge. | A small bump can work. |
| 4. Add one nose at the muzzle's tip. | A dot or small oval is enough. |
| 5. Add one eye inside the head. | Place it behind the nose. |
| 6. Draw one long ear shape above the head. | A narrow leaf can be an ear. |
| 7. Draw one second ear behind it. | It can lean in another direction. |
| 8. Draw one inner-ear shape inside the nearer ear. | Leave room between the two outlines. |
| 9. Draw one curved cheek line below the eye. | A short curve is enough. |
| 10. Draw one large hind-leg oval inside the body's lower left. | Make it broader than a front paw. |
| 11. Draw one long hind-foot shape beneath that oval. | A low rounded shape can point forwards. |
| 12. Draw one short front-leg shape below the body's right side. | Let it reach towards the ground. |
| 13. Draw one front-paw shape at its lower end. | A small flat oval can work. |
| 14. Draw one round tail at the body's left edge. | Let it touch the body. |
| 15. Add one mouth curve below the nose. **Optional.** | Try a very short bend. |
| 16. Draw one whisker from the muzzle. **Optional.** | A single line is enough. |
| 17. Draw one second whisker from the muzzle. **Optional.** | Choose a different angle. |
| 18. Add one toe mark on the hind foot. **Optional.** | A tiny line will do. |
| 19. Draw one ground curve below the rabbit. **Optional.** | Let it pass beneath the feet. |
| 20. Draw one leaf near the rabbit. **Optional.** | Choose where the rabbit might notice it. |

**Parent scaffold:** invite the child to point out the large hind-leg shape in the reference. If requested, compare it with the front leg. Demonstrate an ear movement on separate paper; the child chooses the ear's direction.

**Simplification:** one ear and two simple feet are sufficient. Skip the inner ear, cheek, and optional features; a body, side-facing head, long ear, and tail can carry the idea.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** imagine what caught the rabbit's attention. A sound source, surprising object, or chosen expression can change the story.

**Later transfer:** another day, try a hare facing the opposite way using a new reference. Choose which shape relationships remain useful and which need changing.

## 5. Cup and saucer

**Objective:** notice open oval rims and how a handle and saucer sit around a container.

**Preparation — no drawing:** a parent may place a safe, empty cup and saucer on a stable table, or use a photograph. Use neither hot liquid nor fragile objects within reach if unsuitable.

**Observation — no drawing:** look slightly down at the cup. Notice the oval opening, the handle's empty space, and the saucer showing around the base. Observation is optional; an imagined cup is also welcome.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one wide smile-shaped curve low on the page for the saucer's front edge. | Leave the upper middle open for the cup; do not close an oval. |
| 2. Draw one smaller oval above it for the cup's rim. | Let the rim sit higher than the saucer. |
| 3. Draw one curve down from the rim's left side. | Stop near the saucer's middle. |
| 4. Draw one curve down from the rim's right side. | The cup may narrow towards its base. |
| 5. Join the lower cup edges with one curve. | A shallow bend can make the base. |
| 6. Draw one handle loop beside the cup. | Begin near the rim and return lower down. |
| 7. Draw one smaller loop inside the handle. | Leave some empty space inside. |
| 8. Draw one inner-rim curve inside the top oval. | Follow the back edge loosely. |
| 9. Extend the saucer's left end with one curve towards the cup. | Stop at the cup's left edge; the hidden part stays undrawn. |
| 10. Extend the saucer's right end with one curve towards the cup. | Stop at the cup's right edge; leave the space behind the cup undrawn. |
| 11. Draw one small base arc below the cup. | Keep it close to the bottom curve. |
| 12. Draw one horizontal band across the cup. **Optional.** | A gently curved line can follow its roundness. |
| 13. Draw one second band below it. **Optional.** | Leave a gap of your choosing. |
| 14. Add one decoration shape on the cup. **Optional.** | Choose a shape that matters to you. |
| 15. Draw one steam curve above the cup. **Optional pretend detail.** | This is drawn steam; the real cup stays empty. |
| 16. Draw one second steam curve. **Optional pretend detail.** | It can bend another way. |
| 17. Draw one biscuit shape beside the saucer. **Optional.** | A round or square biscuit can work. |
| 18. If you added a biscuit, add one dot on it. **Optional.** | It could be a raisin. Without a biscuit, you can leave this out. |
| 19. Draw one short table-edge line left of the saucer. **Optional.** | Stop before the saucer's outline. |
| 20. Draw one short table-edge line right of the saucer. **Optional.** | Imagine it continuing behind the saucer. |

**Parent scaffold:** if asked, point to the handle's hole rather than prescribing a perfect loop. Compare the real rim with a circle on separate paper. Avoid moving the observation object while the child wants to look at it.

**Simplification:** draw a cup with one handle curve and a smile-shaped saucer edge. The demonstration leaves hidden saucer edges undrawn from the start. If the child chooses complete shapes or overlapping construction lines, they can keep them; no erasing is required. Decorations, biscuits, steam, and table edges are optional.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** invent who uses this cup and for what imaginary occasion. Change its shape, handle, pattern, or setting to suit that person or creature.

**Later transfer:** on another day, observe an empty jug or bowl. Try its opening and handle or base without the cup demonstration, choosing which parts are visible.

## 6. Bowl of fruit

**Objective:** place overlapping fruit shapes above a container and choose which edges are hidden.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose a broad space. A parent may arrange a safe bowl and fruit, or use a picture; handling or eating the fruit is not required.

**Observation — no drawing:** notice which fruit is in front, where one outline disappears, and how the bowl holds the group together. This proposal puts fruit behind the bowl's front rim.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one shallow U shape for the bowl. | Leave space above the open top. |
| 2. Join its upper ends with one shallow curve. | This can be the front rim. |
| 3. Draw one small base oval beneath the bowl. | Let it touch the bottom. |
| 4. Draw one rounded fruit outline rising above the left rim. | Stop each end at the rim. |
| 5. Draw one pear-shaped outline beside that fruit, stopping where the rim or neighbouring fruit hides it. | Make its top narrow; leave the hidden lower or side edges undrawn. |
| 6. Draw one small stem on the pear. | A little bent line is enough. |
| 7. Draw one orange-shaped outline on the right. | Stop where the bowl hides its bottom. |
| 8. Draw one short banana curve above the other fruit. | Leave room for a second edge. |
| 9. Draw one returning curve beside it to close the banana. | Join near the two tips. |
| 10. Add one short banana-stalk shape at one tip. | A tiny narrow shape can work. |
| 11. Draw one partial fruit arc behind the pear. **Optional.** | Stop where the pear would hide it. |
| 12. Add one short stem on the left fruit. **Optional.** | Put it near that fruit's top. |
| 13. If you added the left fruit's stem, draw one leaf beside it. **Optional.** | A pointed oval can work. Without that stem, you can leave this out. |
| 14. Add one small spot on the orange. **Optional.** | A dot is enough. |
| 15. Add one second spot on the orange. **Optional.** | Choose a different position. |
| 16. Draw one small shine shape on the pear. **Optional.** | A little curved patch can suggest light. |
| 17. Draw one decorative band across the bowl. **Optional.** | Try a curve echoing the rim. |
| 18. If you added a band, add one decoration shape below it. **Optional.** | Invent a pattern shape. Without a band, you can leave this out. |
| 19. Draw one fallen fruit shape beside the bowl. **Optional.** | Leave a gap if it sits separately. |
| 20. If you added fallen fruit, draw one leaf beside it. **Optional.** | Choose its direction. Without fallen fruit, you can leave this out. |

**Parent scaffold:** ask, “Which fruit hides a little of another?” Point to the actual arrangement if requested. Demonstrate a broken outline on separate paper; do not insist that construction lines be erased.

**Simplification:** choose just two fruits and one U-shaped bowl. Overlapping complete outlines are an acceptable exploratory version; partial contours can be revisited another day.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** invent a fruit, redesign the container, or imagine a market stall. A chosen change can alter the arrangement rather than just its colours.

**Later transfer:** another day, arrange two toys partly behind a box. Draw only the visible parts if that interests the child, using the new objects rather than the fruit sequence.

## 7. Tree beside a winding path

**Objective:** explore how a path's edges can come closer together as it travels into a picture.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose which side will hold the tree and leave a broad area for the path. The proposed tree stands on the left.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at a photograph of a path from the ground. Notice whether its visible edges are farther apart nearby and closer together farther away. This is one way to suggest distance, not a compulsory rule for imagined scenes.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one distant hill line across the upper part of the paper. | A low bend can leave room for the scene. |
| 2. Draw one winding path edge from the bottom towards the hill. | Let it turn gently. |
| 3. Draw one second path edge beside it. | Start farther apart below; come closer near the hill. |
| 4. Draw one trunk edge on the path's left. | Leave space above for branches. |
| 5. Draw one second trunk edge beside it. | The trunk can widen near the ground. |
| 6. Draw one branch line from the trunk towards the left. | Let it reach above the path. |
| 7. Draw one branch line from the trunk towards the right. | It can be shorter than the other branch. |
| 8. Draw one large leafy outline around the branch ends. | Bumps, waves, or an uneven oval can work. |
| 9. Draw one root curve from the trunk's left base. | Let it travel a little along the ground. |
| 10. Draw one root curve from the trunk's right base. | Choose another direction. |
| 11. Add one short bark line inside the trunk. **Optional.** | Follow the trunk's length. |
| 12. Draw one leafy cluster inside the crown. **Optional.** | A wavy patch can suggest a clump. |
| 13. Draw one large stone beside the nearby path. **Optional.** | A low rounded shape can work. |
| 14. Draw one smaller stone farther along the path. **Optional.** | Place it nearer the hill. |
| 15. Draw one grass blade near the tree. **Optional.** | A short bent line is enough. |
| 16. Draw one grass blade beside the path. **Optional.** | Leave the path open if you prefer. |
| 17. Draw one small bush near the distant hill. **Optional.** | A little bumpy shape can work. |
| 18. Draw one cloud above the hill. **Optional.** | Leave room around the tree's crown. |
| 19. Draw one fallen leaf near the trunk. **Optional.** | Its shape is up to you. |
| 20. Draw one sun shape in the sky. **Optional.** | A partial sun at an edge can work too. |

**Parent scaffold:** use two fingers above a reference to indicate the path's width at two places. Ask whether the child wants a close or far destination. Show a gentler winding movement separately if requested.

**Simplification:** use a straight path with two edges, a simple trunk, and one crown shape. Equal-width paths and imaginative views are welcome; skip all scene details.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** decide where the path leads, whether it splits, or what season the tree suggests. Alter the route or destination to support that idea.

**Later transfer:** another day, try a river or railway disappearing into a different landscape. Explore changing the space between two edges without using the tree-path model.

## 8. Castle with a drawbridge

**Objective:** arrange connected buildings and use a projecting bridge shape to explore depth.

**Preparation — no drawing:** leave room below the castle for a bridge and moat. The castle can be imagined; a real castle reference is optional.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at the meeting of a gate and bridge in the authored reference. Notice the bridge's far edge at the gate and wider near edge. Towers may differ; matching halves is not required.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one rectangle for the central castle wall. | Leave room on either side for towers. |
| 2. Draw one tall rectangle touching its left side. | This is one possible tower. |
| 3. Draw one tall rectangle touching its right side. | Choose its height yourself. |
| 4. Draw one pointed roof on the left tower. | A triangle can work. |
| 5. Draw one pointed roof on the right tower. | It can have a different shape. |
| 6. Draw one arch shape inside the wall for the gate. | Let its open ends reach the wall's base. |
| 7. Draw one bridge edge from the gate's lower left towards you. | Let it slope outwards on the paper. |
| 8. Draw one bridge edge from the gate's lower right towards you. | Leave a wider gap at the near end. |
| 9. Join the near bridge ends with one line. | This closes the bridge's front edge. |
| 10. Draw one plank line across the bridge. | Keep it between the two sides. |
| 11. Draw one second plank line across the bridge. | Leave some space from the first. |
| 12. Draw one moat curve on the bridge's left. | Stop where the bridge hides the water. |
| 13. Draw one moat curve on the bridge's right. | Imagine the water continuing behind it. |
| 14. Draw one window on the left tower. **Optional.** | A small arch or square can work. |
| 15. Draw one window on the right tower. **Optional.** | Choose another position. |
| 16. Draw one flagpole on a tower roof. **Optional.** | A short upright line is enough. |
| 17. If you added a flagpole, draw one flag shape attached to it. **Optional.** | Imagine a breeze. Without a pole, you can leave this out. |
| 18. Draw one lifting-chain line beside the bridge's left edge. **Optional.** | Join near the gate and the bridge's front corner. |
| 19. Draw one lifting-chain line beside the bridge's right edge. **Optional.** | Use the other corner if you like. |
| 20. Add one small stone shape on the central wall. **Optional.** | One stone can suggest the material. |

**Parent scaffold:** compare the near and far bridge widths on the teacher's reference. A strip of paper can demonstrate a bridge on a separate surface if useful. Ask how the child's imagined gate works; do not impose engineering accuracy.

**Simplification:** draw one wall, one tower, an arch, and a rectangular bridge. Omit chains, moat, roofs, and decorative details as desired. A flat view still communicates a castle.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** invent the castle's inhabitants and a way they cross the moat. An unusual bridge, gate, or tower should be allowed to change the design.

**Later transfer:** on another day, draw a garden gate with a path leading towards it. Try a near and far edge in that new setting without the castle model.

## 9. Lighthouse on the coast

**Objective:** combine a tapered tower with a coastline and distinguish foreground from distant sea.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose where the tower stands and leave a separate area for the sea.

**Observation — no drawing:** look at a lighthouse picture. Notice its base compared with its top and where the land meets the water. Some towers are straight rather than tapered; either is welcome.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one horizon line across the upper half of the page. | This suggests the far edge of the sea. |
| 2. Draw one coastline curve below it. | Leave land on one side of the curve. |
| 3. Draw one tower edge upwards from the land. | Lean it gently towards the tower's middle. |
| 4. Draw one second tower edge beside it. | Leave a wider space at the base if you like. |
| 5. Join the tower edges at the base with one curve. | A shallow bend can suggest a round base. |
| 6. Draw one narrow platform shape across the tower's top. | Let it project a little on either side. |
| 7. Draw one lantern-room rectangle above the platform. | This is the room for the light. |
| 8. Draw one roof shape above that room. | A low triangle or curve can work. |
| 9. Draw one upright window divider inside the lantern room. | A short line is enough. |
| 10. Draw one door near the tower's base. | A small arch can work. |
| 11. Draw one curved stripe across the tower. **Optional.** | Keep it between the two tower edges. |
| 12. Draw one second curved stripe below it. **Optional.** | Leave a gap of your choosing. |
| 13. Draw one light-beam edge from the lantern towards the sea. **Optional.** | A sloping line can suggest its direction. |
| 14. Draw one second light-beam edge from the lantern. **Optional.** | Spread it apart from the first. |
| 15. Draw one rock shape beside the coast. **Optional.** | A lumpy outline is enough. |
| 16. Draw one wave curve on the sea. **Optional.** | Place it away from the land. |
| 17. Draw one second wave curve. **Optional.** | It can be shorter than the first. |
| 18. Draw one cloud in the sky. **Optional.** | Choose where it belongs. |
| 19. Draw one small distant-boat shape near the horizon. **Optional.** | A low crescent can suggest a hull. |
| 20. Draw one path curve from the foreground towards the door. **Optional.** | Let it end near the tower's base. |

**Parent scaffold:** invite the child to indicate land and sea in the reference. If asked, demonstrate two lines leaning towards each other on separate paper. Explain the light-beam lines as a drawing convention, not something that must appear in daylight.

**Simplification:** use a straight rectangular tower, a roof, and one coast curve. Leave out stripes, distant objects, beams, and the path; a lighthouse may be very simple.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** choose daylight, fog, night, or an invented kind of weather. Change the sky, light, or shoreline to express that choice rather than merely reproducing the teacher's scene.

**Later transfer:** another day, try a water tower or windmill in a different landscape. Notice its base, top, and ground contact using a new picture or imagination.

## 10. Underwater coral scene

**Objective:** arrange branching and rounded forms at different positions, with optional overlap and open water between them.

**Preparation — no drawing:** choose a broad area for the seabed and plenty of space above. This is a stylised scene; an aquarium photograph or picture-book reference is optional.

**Observation — no drawing:** notice a branching coral beside a rounded coral or sponge in a reference. Look at gaps between branches and which forms stand in front. Corals have many real forms; these are two possibilities.

| Drawing action | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|
| 1. Draw one gently uneven seabed line low on the page. | Leave open water above it. |
| 2. Draw one rock outline resting on the seabed. | Choose a low rounded shape. |
| 3. Draw one upright coral branch from the seabed's left side. | A slightly bending line can work. |
| 4. Draw one fork from that branch towards the left. | Let it join somewhere along the branch. |
| 5. Draw one fork from that branch towards the right. | Leave a water gap between the forks. |
| 6. Draw one smaller twig from the left fork. | A short line can suggest another branch. |
| 7. Draw one smaller twig from the right fork. | Choose its direction. |
| 8. Draw one rounded coral outline on the seabed's right side. | A lumpy dome is one idea. |
| 9. Draw one curved ridge inside that dome. | Follow a little of its outer shape. |
| 10. Draw one second curved ridge inside the dome. | Let a gap remain between the ridges. |
| 11. Draw one tall seaweed blade behind the rock. | Stop its lower outline where the rock hides it. |
| 12. Draw one second seaweed blade beside it. | It can bend another way. |
| 13. Draw one fish-body oval in the open water. | Leave space around it to swim. |
| 14. Draw one tail shape touching the fish's back end. | A triangle can work. |
| 15. Add one eye inside the fish. | A dot is enough. |
| 16. Draw one fin shape on the fish. **Optional.** | Choose a side or the top. |
| 17. Draw one bubble above the fish. **Optional.** | A small circle can work. |
| 18. Draw one second bubble higher up. **Optional.** | It can have a different size. |
| 19. Draw one shell shape on the seabed. **Optional.** | A little fan outline is one possibility. |
| 20. Add one stripe across the fish. **Optional.** | Keep it inside the body if you wish. |

**Parent scaffold:** when asked, point to the spaces between coral branches and help choose a comfortable branch size. Demonstrate a single fork on separate paper. Avoid turning real coral variety into a requirement for one correct outline.

**Simplification:** keep one branch, one dome, and a fish made from an oval and tail. Skip partial outlines, ridges, bubbles, and shell. There is no need to fill the page.

**Creative exploration — no prescribed marks:** choose the scene's resident or invent a coral form. Alter its scale, shelter, or relationship with another creature to build a story; biological realism is optional in this imaginative drawing.

**Later transfer:** another day, draw a rock pool or small garden with both branching and rounded forms. Find a new arrangement and decide which empty spaces or overlaps to keep, without the underwater demonstration.

## Review checklist before these become lessons

- Confirm that every demonstrated outline matches its one-action instruction and optional hint, including where visible edges stop at an overlap. Plan hidden gaps before playback so the demonstration never relies on erasing. Reveal no future marks during a turn.
- Check optional dependencies: the family can explicitly skip a dependent detail when its earlier feature is absent. The instructions offer that choice; no automatic transition or assumption about the child's paper is permitted.
- Check that optional omissions remain understandable and require no corrective judgement of the child's paper.
- Review animal anatomy simplifications, spatial arrangements, language, and visual access with an art educator; revise ambiguous references to left/right or hidden edges.
- Test reference legibility, replay, motor access, narration, and parent help burden with families of varied experience. A family can choose an easier variation or stop.
- Preserve the same privacy and explicit-control boundaries during tests. Any research collection of children's work or experiences requires a separate, proportionate consent and data-handling design.
- Evaluate later independent attempts separately from copying. Keep the label Expert attached to the challenge collection, never to a child's identity or status.
