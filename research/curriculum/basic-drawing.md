# Basic drawing: ten shared paper activities

Content design, 26 October 2026. These are authored lesson scripts for a parent-supervised paper-drawing experience. “Basic” describes the available challenge, not the child. There are no age bands, entry tests, required likenesses, or mastery claims.

## Evidence and delivery contract

The curriculum combines brief demonstration, observation, supported attempts, and creative choices. That combination is evidence-informed; these subjects, exact words, sequences, and turn counts have **not** been validated as an effective protocol. An art educator should review them, followed by supervised family usability and learning evaluation.

- Observation coaching is supported by a small drawing experiment; generalisation and retention remain uncertain. Each activity therefore offers one concrete noticing cue rather than constant questions. See [drawing evidence, lines 5–13](../drawing-instruction-evidence.md#1-observation-coaching-small-randomized-experiment).
- Cube-drawing research distinguishes copying performance from transfer and favours attention to meaningful parts over stroke order alone. The scripts name relationships; the precise sequence is a design choice. See [drawing evidence, lines 15–22](../drawing-instruction-evidence.md#2-copying-tracing-and-transfer-four-experiments).
- Guided exploration and explicit instruction have complementary, task-dependent evidence; no universal method wins. Creative choices must change the picture. See [research synthesis, lines 47–55](../teaching-methodology-review.md#3-combine-clear-teaching-with-guided-exploration).
- Parents can discuss identifying features and demonstrate separately, without taking over the child's marks. Direct drawing evidence here is observational, not proof that supervision alone improves learning. See [drawing evidence, lines 24–31](../drawing-instruction-evidence.md#3-parentchild-collaborative-drawing-observational-mechanism-evidence).
- Revisiting a movement in a different picture is a proposed adaptation of narrow graphomotor-practice findings. No prescribed repetition quota follows from those studies. See [practice evidence, lines 13–19](../pacing-practice-medium-evidence.md#practice-quantity-versus-retention).

**For every activity:** the adult stays present. Paper is the child's drawing surface; the screen provides an optional demonstration and a still reference. The child may use a pencil, crayon, or other comfortable drawing tool. Spoken instructions never claim to see or evaluate the paper.

Preparation, observation, and exploration are separate from numbered drawing actions. Read the short instruction at each action; offer its hint only if requested. Then remain silent until the family explicitly chooses Next, Again, Help, Skip for optional details, or Finish. No timer, silence detector, demonstration completion, or voice response advances the activity. There is unlimited waiting and repetition. Any activity can finish early; unmarked actions are the suggested core, not a compulsory completion test.

If an optional mark relies on a detail the child left out, the family can explicitly choose Next or Skip without drawing it. Conditional instructions below offer that choice; they never require adding the omitted detail, inspect the paper, or trigger automatic omission or progression.

Counts below cover the core prepared script: one preparation turn, one observation turn, the listed drawing actions, and one exploration turn. Optional hints and requested repetitions add turns without a limit. No timing target is implied.

| Activity | Drawing actions | Core teaching turns, including all optional actions |

## 1. Curious cat face

**Objective:** combine a rounded outline with positioned features and attached lines.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Find your paper and a drawing tool with your grown-up. Choose Next when you are ready.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Have a look at a cat picture together. Where do its ears join its head?” A photograph, book picture, or the screen reference is suitable; no live animal interaction is needed.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one round shape for the head. | Bring a slow curve around towards where you started. A gap is fine. |
| 2 | Add one ear at the top left. | Try one pointy shape or a soft bend. |
| 3 | Add one ear at the top right. | This ear can be different from the first. |
| 4 | Put one eye inside the left side of the head. | A small dot can be an eye. |
| 5 | Put one eye inside the right side of the head. | A little curve could make a sleepy eye. |
| 6 | Add a little nose below the eyes. | Try a dot or a tiny triangle. |
| 7 | Add a curved mouth below the nose. | One little bend is enough. |
| 8 — optional | Draw one whisker out from the left cheek. | Start near the cheek and move outwards. |
| 9 — optional | Add another whisker on the left. | Put one line beside the first. |
| 10 — optional | Add one more left whisker, if you like. | It can be short, long, or curly. |
| 11 — optional | Draw one whisker out from the right cheek. | Move outwards from the other cheek. |
| 12 — optional | Add another whisker on the right. | Leave a little room beside the first. |
| 13 — optional | Add one more right whisker, if you like. | The sides do not need to match. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “Would your cat like a hat, an imaginary home, or something you invent? You can explore or finish here.”

**Parent scaffolding:** point out left and right on the reference if useful. Demonstrate an ear on your own sheet if requested. Do not correct the child's expression or insist on matching whisker counts.

**Later transfer invitation:** another time, offer a different animal face without the same demonstration: “What shapes could help you make a bear?” Keep replay available if wanted.

## 2. Friendly sun

**Objective:** place outward lines around a central shape.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Settle your paper with your grown-up. Choose a drawing tool you like.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at the sun drawing on the screen. Notice how the rays go out from the middle.” Use the illustration; never ask anyone to look at the real sun.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one round shape for the sun. | Make it a size that leaves some paper around it. |
| 2 | Draw one ray upwards from the sun. | A ray can be one short straight line. |
| 3 | Draw one ray downwards from the sun. | Start near the lower edge and move out. |
| 4 | Draw one ray out to the left. | Your grown-up can point to the left on the example. |
| 5 | Draw one ray out to the right. | Move away from the round shape. |
| 6 | Add one slanting ray in a gap. | Choose any empty space around the sun. |
| 7 | Add one more slanting ray. | It does not need to match the others. |
| 8 — optional | Add another ray wherever you choose. | A wavy line can be a ray too. |
| 9 — optional | Add one last ray, if you like. | Choose a gap or leave this detail out. |
| 10 — optional | Give the sun one eye. | A dot can do the job. |
| 11 — optional | Give the sun another eye. | Put it where you want the face to be. |
| 12 — optional | Add a curved mouth. | Choose a smile, a sleepy mouth, or your own expression. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “Would you like a rainbow-coloured sun or something surprising beside it? You can choose your own idea or finish.”

**Parent scaffolding:** model one outward movement on a separate sheet. Rays need no even spacing, matching lengths, or set total. The face is an imaginative option, not an observation of the real sun.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could lines going out from a middle help you draw a spark or a dandelion?” Offer a suitable reference if requested.

## 3. Rainbow

**Objective:** make neighbouring arches and notice inside/outside relationships.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Put your paper somewhere comfortable. Choose a pencil or a colour with your grown-up.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at the rainbow example. Notice how one arch sits inside another.”

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one big arch. | Move up, curve over, and come down in one flowing line. |
| 2 | Draw a smaller arch inside it. | Leave a little space between the two lines. |
| 3 | Add another arch inside the last one. | You can follow the bend slowly. |
| 4 | Add one more inner arch. | Make the bend smaller if you need room. |
| 5 — optional | Add another arch, if there is room. | You may choose a new colour before you start. |
| 6 — optional | Add one more inner arch. | A wobbly bend is welcome. |
| 7 — optional | Add another little inner arch, if you like. | You can skip this one if the space feels small. |
| 8 — optional | Add a final inner arch, if you have room. | Leave a little opening below your smallest curve. |
| 9 — optional | Draw one fluffy cloud near an end. | Join some small bumps into one cloud outline. |
| 10 — optional | Draw another cloud near the other end. | This cloud can have its own shape. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “What might be under your rainbow? Choose your own place, or finish here.”

**Parent scaffolding:** demonstrate an arch in the air. Offer a bigger first arch if the child requests more room. Seven colours, exact colour order, even spacing, and cloud symmetry are unnecessary for this movement activity; an imagined rainbow may use any colours.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could a big arch and a smaller arch make an entrance to a tunnel?” Invite an independent attempt another day.

## 4. Floating balloon

**Objective:** connect a rounded outline, a small knot and a flowing line; colour with short strokes inside a boundary while leaving white space for shine.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Settle paper, a pencil and the crayons you have, leaving room for a bending string.” Adult setup is completed before this lesson begins; no real balloons or loose strings are required.

**Observation — spoken:** “Let's draw a balloon for a pretend paper celebration. Notice its big rounded shape, little knot and bending string.”

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one tall, rounded shape with room below. | A wide or wobbly balloon belongs in your picture too. |
| 2 | Join a tiny triangle to the bottom. | Let the triangle touch the bottom of your rounded shape. |
| 3 | Let a bending string travel down from the knot. | Look at the knot before beginning your long bending line. |
| 4 — optional | Try a small bow loop beside the knot, if you like. | Bring one curved loop back to where it began. |
| 5 — optional | Try a bow loop on the other side, if you like. | Each loop joins the knot; either loop can stand alone. |
| 6 — optional | Add a little shine curve inside, if you like. | Keep this small curve inside your big rounded outline. |
| 7 — optional | Add a dot inside your balloon, if you like. | Find a spot inside the outline, away from its edge. |
| 8 — optional | Choose another place for a dot, if you like. | Leave a little room around each dot you choose. |
| 9 — optional | Add a last dot, or keep your spaces. | Your pattern can have one dot, many dots or none. |

The reviewed teaching pack adds two required red-body colouring demonstrations with short curved patches, pen lifts and inset edges. The upper-left shine stays white. Yellow bow colouring is optional and explicitly depends only on a bow the child chose to add; the tiny knot stays white. Any available colour can replace the example's red or yellow. A brief whole-picture reflection leads into open exploration. Music, drawing and speech use the trusted scripted sequence; neither audio nor the model decides progression.

**Exploration — spoken:** “What could join your paper celebration? Try another balloon or something you invent. Keep drawing as long as you like, or finish.”

**Parent scaffolding:** demonstrate a bending line on your own paper if help is wanted. Let the child choose the balloon's shape and pattern. The knot communicates attachment without requiring exact geometry. Pause, replay or use More time whenever helpful; the timing is an adjustable design estimate, not a learning deadline.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could a bending line become a kite's tail another day?” Use a picture of a kite and let the child choose its shape.

## 5. Garden flower

**Objective:** arrange parts around a centre and connect a stem to a flower head.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Set out your paper and drawing tool with your grown-up. Leave room below your flower.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at a flower picture together. Where do its petals meet?” A picture or an adult-chosen safe specimen is suitable; no picking or tasting is part of the activity.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one small round centre. | Leave some paper around it for petals. |
| 2 | Draw one petal above the centre. | Make one curved loop that comes back to the middle. |
| 3 | Draw one petal beside the centre. | Choose a side with some space. |
| 4 | Add one petal below the centre. | The petal can be short or long. |
| 5 | Add one petal on the other side. | Let its loop touch the centre if you want it joined. |
| 6 — optional | Add another petal in a gap. | Choose a space that feels comfortable to draw in. |
| 7 — optional | Add one more petal, if you like. | Flowers do not all have the same number. |
| 8 | Draw a stem down from the flower head. | One long line can be a stem. |
| 9 — optional | Add one leaf touching the stem. | A pointed oval can make a leaf. |
| 10 — optional | Add another leaf wherever you choose. | It can be bigger or smaller than the first. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “What kind of garden would you invent? You could add your own plant, or finish here.”

**Parent scaffolding:** trace the centre-to-petal relationship on the reference with a finger. Demonstrate a loop separately if requested. Do not insist on radial symmetry, a fixed petal count, or matching a particular species.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could loops around a middle help you invent a different flower?” Offer a contrasting flower reference another time.

## 6. Apple

**Objective:** build an uneven rounded outline from two curves and attach a small feature.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Put your paper beside your grown-up. Choose a drawing tool you can move comfortably.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at an apple or a picture of one. Notice the little dip where its stalk starts.” An adult handles any real object; eating is separate from drawing.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw a curve for the left side of the apple. | Start near the top and curve outwards towards the bottom. |
| 2 | Draw a curve for the right side. | Bring it towards the ends of the first curve. Gaps are fine. |
| 3 | Add a little stalk at the top. | One short curved line can be a stalk. |
| 4 — optional | Add one leaf touching the stalk. | Try a small pointed oval. |
| 5 — optional | If you added a leaf, draw one line through it, or skip. | Follow from the leaf's base towards its tip. Without a leaf, you can leave this out. |
| 6 — optional | Add one curved shine mark inside the apple. | Make a short bend near one side. |
| 7 — optional | Add one little curve at the top for the dip. | Put a shallow bend near the stalk. |
| 8 — optional | Draw one ground line below the apple. | A short line can suggest a surface. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “Would you like a patterned apple, a different colour, or an idea of your own? You can also finish.”

**Parent scaffolding:** show the two sides on a real apple or reference, rather than correcting the child's outline. Offer a simpler single oval if combining curves feels unwelcome. A coloured or patterned invention is as acceptable as an observed version.

**Later transfer invitation:** “How might those two curves change for a pear?” Compare an apple and pear reference another day without requiring a likeness.

## 7. Little fish

**Objective:** join differently shaped parts and direct a line away from a body.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Find your paper and a comfortable drawing tool with your grown-up.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at a fish picture. Notice how its tail joins its body.”

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one oval for the fish's body. | A wide round shape can work too. |
| 2 | Add one tail at an end of the body. | Try a triangle with its point meeting the body. |
| 3 | Put one eye near the other end. | A little dot can be an eye. |
| 4 | Add a small mouth near the eye. | Try one short curved line. |
| 5 — optional | Add one fin above the body. | A little triangle can join the upper edge. |
| 6 — optional | Add one fin below the body. | This fin can have a different shape. |
| 7 — optional | Draw one curved gill line behind the eye. | Make a short bend inside the body. |
| 8 — optional | Add one curved scale mark inside the body. | A small curve is enough. |
| 9 — optional | Add another scale mark. | Choose any space inside the body. |
| 10 — optional | Add one more scale mark, if you like. | Your pattern does not need even rows. |
| 11 — optional | Draw one bubble beside the fish. | Use a small round shape. |
| 12 — optional | Add another bubble wherever you choose. | It can be a different size. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “What might live beside your fish? You could invent a neighbour, or finish here.”

**Parent scaffolding:** discuss the tail-body join using the reference. The eye position determines the facing direction; no correction is needed if the child invents another arrangement. Offer a simple tail shape separately if requested.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could an oval and a different tail make another swimming creature?” Let the family choose a reference or an imaginary creature.

## 8. Ladybird

**Objective:** divide an enclosed shape and attach short lines around it.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Settle your paper with your grown-up. Leave a little room around your ladybird.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at a ladybird picture. Notice the line between the two wing covers.” Use a picture rather than collecting insects.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one oval for the body. | A round shape is welcome too. |
| 2 | Add one small head at the top. | Try a rounded bump touching the body. |
| 3 | Draw one line down the middle of the body. | Start near the head and move towards the bottom. |
| 4 — optional | Add one feeler from the head. | Draw a short bend outwards. |
| 5 — optional | Add another feeler from the head. | It can curl a different way. |
| 6 — optional | Draw one spot inside the body. | Use a little round mark. |
| 7 — optional | Add another spot wherever you choose. | Ladybird spots can have different sizes. |
| 8 — optional | Add one more spot, if you like. | Choose a space inside the body. |
| 9 — optional | Draw one leg out from the left side. | A short line is enough for a leg. |
| 10 — optional | Add another leg on the left. | Choose a space beside the first. |
| 11 — optional | Add one more leg on the left. | It can point a different way. |
| 12 — optional | Draw one leg out from the right side. | Start near the body's edge. |
| 13 — optional | Add another leg on the right. | Make one short line outwards. |
| 14 — optional | Add one more leg on the right, if you like. | The lines do not need to match the other side. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “Where could your ladybird be exploring? You could invent a place, or finish here.”

**Parent scaffolding:** the reference can show six legs without turning the child's drawing into a counting test. Skipped legs or imaginative features are not failures. Offer your own simple dividing-line demonstration if requested.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could a big body shape with parts around it help you invent a beetle?” Compare a new reference on another occasion.

## 9. Ice-cream cone

**Objective:** join a rounded top to a pointed base and place optional marks inside a boundary.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Find your paper and drawing tool with your grown-up. Leave room for a scoop above the cone.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at the ice-cream drawing. Notice where the round scoop sits on the cone.” No food or tasting is needed.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw a big V for the cone. | Make one line down to a point and back up. |
| 2 | Draw one rounded scoop across the top. | Join the upper ends of the V with a big curved bump. |
| 3 — optional | Draw one small cherry shape on the scoop. | A little circle can be a cherry. |
| 4 — optional | If you added a cherry, give it one little stalk, or skip. | Use one short curved line. Without a cherry, you can leave this out. |
| 5 — optional | Draw one sprinkle inside the scoop. | A sprinkle can be a tiny dash. |
| 6 — optional | Add another sprinkle. | Choose a different direction if you like. |
| 7 — optional | Add one more sprinkle wherever you choose. | Leave it out if you prefer a plain scoop. |
| 8 — optional | Draw one slanting line inside the cone. | Stop near its edges. Going over an edge is fine too. |
| 9 — optional | Add another slanting line inside the cone. | Leave a little space beside the first. |
| 10 — optional | If you added slanting lines, draw one line across them, or skip. | Cross the slanting lines in another direction. Without those lines, you can leave this out. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “What imaginary flavour or topping would you invent? You can add your idea or finish.”

**Parent scaffolding:** explain the scoop-cone join on the reference. A wide V is an easier option when the cone feels cramped. The single continuous V is one action; demonstrate it separately on request rather than taking the child's tool.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could a pointed base and a rounded top help you invent a different object?” Let the child choose, or suggest a party hat with a pompom.

## 10. Woodland mushroom

**Objective:** connect a broad cap to a narrower stalk and explore curved boundaries.

**Preparation — spoken:** “Set out your paper with your grown-up. Leave some room below the mushroom cap.”

**Observation — spoken:** “Look at a mushroom picture. Notice how the cap is wider than the stalk.” Use a picture; finding, picking, touching, or tasting wild mushrooms is not part of this activity.

| Action | Child-facing instruction | Optional hint on request |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one broad arch for the cap. | Move up, curve across, and come down. |
| 2 | Join the arch's ends with one shallow curve. | Make a gentle bend across the open bottom. |
| 3 | Draw one side of the stalk down from the cap. | Start under the cap, a little away from its edge. |
| 4 | Draw the other side of the stalk. | Leave some space beside the first line. |
| 5 | Join the bottom of the stalk with a curve. | A small bend can connect the two sides. |
| 6 — optional | Add one curved line inside the cap's lower edge. | Follow a little of the shallow bottom curve. |
| 7 — optional | Draw one spot on the cap. | A small round shape can be a spot. |
| 8 — optional | Add another spot on the cap. | This one can be a different size. |
| 9 — optional | Add another spot wherever you choose. | Leave room around it if you like. |
| 10 — optional | Add one last spot, if you want. | An uneven shape can be a spot too. |
| 11 — optional | Draw one blade of grass near the stalk. | One short upward line can suggest grass. |
| 12 — optional | Add another blade of grass. | Try a different direction or length. |

**Exploration — spoken:** “Would your mushroom be tiny, enormous, or a home in an imaginary woodland? Explore your idea or finish.”

**Parent scaffolding:** show cap width and stalk width on the reference with your fingers. Offer a single-line stalk as a simpler alternative. Spots are an imaginative option, not a guide to identifying edible mushrooms.

**Later transfer invitation:** “Could a wide curved top and a narrow part below help you draw an umbrella?” Offer a new reference and allow any approach.

## Review and evaluation notes

The counts describe authored content, not a developmental norm or a requirement to finish. The child can choose a different shape, decline a detail, request another demonstration, or stop at any boundary. Do not judge talent, effort, mood, correctness, or improvement from unseen paper. A parent may ask what a mark means and accept the child's explanation.

Before publication, review demonstrations and words together: every demonstration must show the named single action and preserve the previous still state. Optional details need clear skip controls. Check that instructions remain comprehensible with narration off and that the parent can operate controls without taking over the drawing activity.

For learning evaluation, distinguish immediate supported drawing from later independent application. Use related new subjects and delayed opportunities, with enjoyment, agency, comfort, and parent workload assessed separately. None of these activities alone establishes general drawing ability or transfer. See [research synthesis, lines 144–154](../teaching-methodology-review.md#evaluation-before-efficacy-claims).
