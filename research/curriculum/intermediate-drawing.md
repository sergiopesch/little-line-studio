# Intermediate drawing curriculum — ten authored paper lessons

Content design, 26 October 2026. Status: proposed authored sequences for educator, family and visual review. These particular lessons and their ordering have not been validated experimentally. “Intermediate” describes the proposed drawing content; it is not an age, ability diagnosis or promise of mastery.

## Shared lesson contract

A parent or responsible adult stays alongside throughout. The website demonstrates; the child draws on their own paper. Each numbered row below is one drawing action and one possible teaching turn. Show only that action, then wait in silence. Progress occurs only when the family chooses **Next**. There is unlimited quiet time, and repetition has no penalty. A hint is offered only on request; it is not an additional automatic instruction. Do not infer what the child drew, how they feel or whether an action is complete.

Preparation, observation and exploration are separate from the numbered drawing actions. Each is one short, family-controlled turn. An observation cue refers to the shared demonstration, or to a suitable reference the parent has chosen; it does not claim to see the child's paper. Optional drawing details are marked **Optional**. Families may skip them, stop early or choose a different representation. When an optional action depends on another optional detail, its wording explicitly lets the family skip if that detail is absent. The website must not infer that it was drawn or progress automatically. No correction is required to match a template. A later transfer invitation is outside the current lesson, never a compulsory test.

Shape words below name a single outline: an oval or rectangle can be made as one continuous outline, with wobbles and gaps welcome. Demonstrations must not silently turn a row into several separate drawing tasks. Where one feature needs two distinct lines, it has two numbered turns. The content is intended for ordinary pencils; colouring and materials beyond paper and pencil remain optional exploration.

## Evidence informing the design

- **R1 — purposeful observation:** brief observation coaching improved detail and conceptual accuracy in a small kindergarten experiment. This supports trying observation cues, without establishing these exact sequences or broad transfer. [Vlach & Carver, 2008](https://ecrp.illinois.edu/v10n1/vlach.html).
- **R2 — relationships and independence:** cube-drawing experiments distinguished accurate copying from later independent drawing. Rigid stroke ordering did not consistently transfer; meaningful faces and relationships were useful. The sequences here are optional scaffolds, not required formulas. [Bremner et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10953929/).
- **R3 — parent collaboration:** discussion of identifying features during mother–child drawing was associated with changes in young children's representations. This is observational evidence, not proof that parent presence alone causes learning. [Braswell & Callanan, 2003](https://digitalcommons.wayne.edu/mpq/vol49/iss4/5/).
- **R4 — guidance with agency:** guided shape play supported conceptual learning in an adjacent task; it does not validate a drawing curriculum. The genuine choices here are hypotheses to evaluate. [Fisher et al., 2013](https://templeinfantlab.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2019/06/Fisher_et_al-2013-Child_Development.pdf).

The repository's [teaching methodology review](../teaching-methodology-review.md) records wider evidence and limitations. Teaching-turn counts are planning decisions, not research-derived attention limits. No timers, praise schedule or speed target is prescribed.

## intermediate-sitting-dog — A dog taking a rest

**Objective:** connect head, body and supporting limbs to suggest a sitting pose; place small facial features inside a larger shape.

**Preparation turn:** “Put your paper somewhere comfortable beside your grown-up.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at the sitting dog in our example. Which part is higher: the head or the paws?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one round shape for the head near the top of your paper. | Leave room below for the body. |
| 2 | Add one hanging curve for an ear on the left. | Start beside the head and curl downwards. |
| 3 | Add one hanging curve for an ear on the right. | This ear can be a different length. |
| 4 | Draw one tall oval below the head for the body. | Let the oval meet the bottom of the head. |
| 5 | Draw one line down the left side of the chest for a front leg. | Start inside the body and move towards its bottom. |
| 6 | Draw one line down the right side of the chest for another front leg. | Leave a little space between the legs. |
| 7 | Add one small curve at the bottom of the left leg for a paw. | Bend the line outwards and back. |
| 8 | Add one small curve at the bottom of the right leg for a paw. | A soft bump is enough. |
| 9 | Draw one rounded bend beside the lower left body for a folded back leg. | Let the bend sit beside the front paw. |
| 10 | Draw one rounded bend beside the lower right body for the other back leg. | It can be smaller than the first. |
| 11 | Draw one tail curving out from the lower body. | Choose a side and curl the line outwards. |
| 12 | Put one eye inside the left side of the head. | A pencil dot can be an eye. |
| 13 | Put one eye inside the right side of the head. | Your eyes do not need to match. |
| 14 | Draw one little nose below the eyes. | Try a small rounded triangle. |
| 15 | Draw one short line down from the nose. | Stop well before the bottom of the head. |
| 16 | Add one little mouth curve to the left of that line. | Start at the line's lower end. |
| 17 | Add one little mouth curve to the right of that line. | A gentle bend can suggest a smile. |
| 18 | **Optional:** draw one curved collar line across the neck. | A shallow curve can follow the bottom of the head. |
| 19 | **Optional:** add one patch shape anywhere on the body. | Choose a shape your dog might have. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if help is requested, point to how the example's chest sits above its front paws. Offer the requested hint or demonstrate on your own paper. Do not move the child's hand or require equal legs.

**Research basis:** R1–R3 inform this relationship-focused support.

**Creative choice:** the child chooses ear lengths, the direction and curl of the tail, and whether the dog has a patch. These alter its character, not just its colour.

**Exploration turn:** “What might your resting dog be thinking about? Add your own idea, keep drawing quietly, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** on another day, invite a sitting rabbit using a head, body and paws, with different ears. Begin without the dog example; offer help if requested.

## intermediate-turtle — A turtle with a patterned shell

**Objective:** attach a head, feet and tail to a large shell; explore how lines divide a surface into areas.

**Preparation turn:** “Make some space for your paper and pencil beside your grown-up.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our turtle. Which small parts stick out beyond its large shell?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one wide oval for the shell. | Stretch the round shape sideways. |
| 2 | Draw one small oval touching the shell's right side for the head. | Let the two outlines meet. |
| 3 | Add one rounded foot below the shell near the head. | A little U-shaped bend can be a foot. |
| 4 | Add one rounded foot below the shell near its left end. | Leave space between the feet. |
| 5 | Draw one pointed bend outside the shell's left end for a tail. | Move out to a point and back to the shell. |
| 6 | Put one eye on the head. | A dot can be enough. |
| 7 | Draw one little mouth curve on the head. | Keep it separate from the eye. |
| 8 | Draw one shallow curve across the shell near its bottom. | Follow the shell's lower edge from inside it. |
| 9 | Draw one small many-sided shape in the middle of the shell. | An uneven shape with several corners can work. |
| 10 | Draw one line from its upper left side towards the shell's upper left edge. | Let the line meet the outer shell. |
| 11 | Draw one line from its upper right side towards the shell's upper right edge. | Choose a different starting spot this time. |
| 12 | Draw one line from its right side towards the shell's right edge. | Travel across the space beside the middle shape. |
| 13 | Draw one line from its lower right side towards the shell's lower edge. | Stop at the lower shell curve if you meet it. |
| 14 | Draw one line from its lower left side towards the shell's lower edge. | This area can be a different size. |
| 15 | Draw one line from its left side towards the shell's left edge. | There is no need for equal spaces. |
| 16 | **Optional:** add one short toe line on the front foot. | A tiny mark can divide the foot. |
| 17 | **Optional:** add one short toe line on the back foot. | Keep the mark inside the foot. |
| 18 | **Optional:** draw one ground line underneath the feet. | Leave the turtle above the line. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if the shell divisions become confusing, offer the simpler choice of a plain shell. At a requested pause, compare the middle shape's edge and the outer shell in the example. The many-sided centre need not have a fixed number of corners. Do not turn shell patterning into counting or correction.

**Research basis:** R1–R3 inform the prompts; the particular pattern is authored.

**Creative choice:** the shell may be plain, divided into irregular areas, or carry a different pattern. The child may make a long or short tail.

**Exploration turn:** “Would your turtle like a pond, a path, or a place you invent? Explore on your paper, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** try a beetle another day: keep a large central body, but choose different parts around its edge. Invite the child to decide which turtle features no longer belong.

## intermediate-butterfly — Wings with their own pattern

**Objective:** arrange wings around a central body; compare left and right without requiring exact symmetry.

**Preparation turn:** “Place your paper where you and your grown-up can both see it.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at the butterfly in our example. Where do the wings join the narrow body?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one thin, upright oval for the body. | Leave space on both sides. |
| 2 | Draw one small round head touching the top of the body. | The head can be wider than the body. |
| 3 | Draw one large looping wing on the upper left. | Start beside the upper body and loop back to its middle. |
| 4 | Draw one large looping wing on the upper right. | Leave room below it for another wing. |
| 5 | Draw one smaller looping wing on the lower left. | Join the middle of the body to its lower end. |
| 6 | Draw one smaller looping wing on the lower right. | The two sides can be different. |
| 7 | Add one antenna curving up from the left side of the head. | A light bend is enough. |
| 8 | Add one antenna curving up from the right side of the head. | It can curl a different way. |
| 9 | Draw one line from the body into the upper left wing. | Stop inside the wing. |
| 10 | Draw one line from the body into the upper right wing. | Let the line spread outwards. |
| 11 | Draw one line from the body into the lower left wing. | Start near where this wing joins the body. |
| 12 | Draw one line from the body into the lower right wing. | The lines need not be the same length. |
| 13 | **Optional:** put one oval inside the upper left wing. | Leave some space around the oval. |
| 14 | **Optional:** put one shape of your choice inside the upper right wing. | If you added an oval on the other wing, this shape may match it or differ. |
| 15 | **Optional:** add one dot inside the lower left wing. | Choose any spot inside it. |
| 16 | **Optional:** add one dot inside the lower right wing. | Choose any spot inside this wing. |
| 17 | **Optional:** draw one short band across the body. | Stop when you reach its other side. |
| 18 | **Optional:** if you added a body band, draw one more band, or skip. | Leave a gap beside an earlier band if you chose one. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if asked, point out the example's wing attachment points rather than comparing the child's wing sizes. Offer to draw a different wing shape on your own paper. Matching and non-matching patterns are equally available.

**Research basis:** R1–R4 inform observing, showing and leaving meaningful choice.

**Creative choice:** rounded, pointed or wavy wings; matching or different patterns on each side. Explain that the demonstration is one possible arrangement.

**Exploration turn:** “Choose your butterfly's own wing pattern, imagine somewhere it could fly, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** draw a moth another day with the same idea of a central body and attached wings, but choose a different outline. The family may look at a parent-selected moth reference if desired.

## intermediate-owl — An owl on a branch

**Objective:** place paired features inside a larger form and connect a creature to a supporting branch.

**Preparation turn:** “Get comfortable with your paper beside your grown-up.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our owl on its branch. Which parts help show where it is sitting?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one tall, rounded outline for the owl's body. | Leave room underneath for a branch. |
| 2 | Add one small pointed bend at the top left for a feather tuft. | Let the bend meet the outer outline. |
| 3 | Add one small pointed bend at the top right for another tuft. | This tuft can point a different way. |
| 4 | Draw one round eye near the top left inside the body. | Leave space for another eye beside it. |
| 5 | Draw one round eye near the top right inside the body. | The eyes may be different sizes. |
| 6 | Put one pupil inside the left eye. | A small dot can be a pupil. |
| 7 | Put one pupil inside the right eye. | Choose where this eye is looking. |
| 8 | Draw one small beak between and below the eyes. | A downward-pointing triangle can work. |
| 9 | Draw one long wing curve inside the body's left side. | Follow the outside edge with some space beside it. |
| 10 | Draw one long wing curve inside the body's right side. | Bring the curve towards the lower body. |
| 11 | Draw one branch line underneath the owl. | Let it reach beyond both sides of the body. |
| 12 | Draw one more branch line below the first. | A wobbly line can make a natural branch. |
| 13 | Draw one short leg line down from the lower left body. | Let it reach the branch. |
| 14 | Draw one short leg line down from the lower right body. | Leave a gap between the legs. |
| 15 | Add one little claw curve where the left leg meets the branch. | Curl the line round the branch's upper edge. |
| 16 | Add one little claw curve where the right leg meets the branch. | A small bend can suggest a gripping claw. |
| 17 | **Optional:** draw one small V-shaped feather on the belly. | Keep the bend away from the eyes. |
| 18 | **Optional:** if you added a belly feather, draw one more, or skip. | Leave space beside an earlier feather if you chose one. |
| 19 | **Optional:** if you added belly feathers, draw one more, or skip. | Any feathers you choose may point different ways. |
| 20 | **Optional:** add one little leaf shape to the branch. | Join the leaf to a branch line. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** call the pointed shapes feather tufts, not ears. If support is requested, discuss the connection between legs, claws and branch. Keep pupil placement the child's choice; do not claim the owl or the child's picture expresses a particular feeling.

**Research basis:** R1–R3 inform this support.

**Creative choice:** choose a wide or slender owl, a direction for its gaze, and a plain or patterned belly.

**Exploration turn:** “What might be beside your owl's branch? Add an idea of your own, keep exploring, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** try a different bird sitting on a fence. Explore which features can stay and which might change, without requiring the owl example.

## intermediate-sailboat — A boat with two sails

**Objective:** use shared edges and attachment points to arrange a hull, mast and sails; distinguish the boat from the water around it.

**Preparation turn:** “Place your paper sideways if you would like a wide sea.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at the sails in our example. Which edge sits beside the upright mast?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one long line across the lower part of the paper for the deck. | Leave space below it for the hull. |
| 2 | Draw one deep curve from the deck's left end to its right end for the hull. | Let the curve dip underneath the deck. |
| 3 | Draw one upright mast line from the middle of the deck. | Leave room near the top of the paper. |
| 4 | Draw one sloping line from the mast's top down towards the left. | Stop above the deck. |
| 5 | Draw one line from that lower end back to the mast. | This closes one sail shape. |
| 6 | Draw one sloping line from the mast's top down towards the right. | This sail can be smaller. |
| 7 | Draw one line from that lower end back to the mast. | Stop when it meets the upright line. |
| 8 | Draw one little round porthole inside the hull. | Leave the round shape below the deck. |
| 9 | Draw one more porthole inside the hull. | Choose a different spot along the boat. |
| 10 | Draw one short curved line inside the bow at the boat's right end. | Let it follow part of the hull's curve. |
| 11 | Draw one wavy water line underneath the boat. | Keep the wave below the hull. |
| 12 | **Optional:** draw one small flag outline beside the top of the mast. | A small pointed loop can meet the mast. |
| 13 | **Optional:** add one more wave near the first. | You can make a different-sized bend. |
| 14 | **Optional:** draw one sun shape above the sea. | Choose a space away from the sails. |
| 15 | **Optional:** draw one cloud outline above the sea. | A soft, bumpy loop can be a cloud. |
| 16 | **Optional:** draw one small bird-wing curve in the sky. | A little upward bend is enough. |
| 17 | **Optional:** if you added a bird-wing curve, add its other wing, or skip. | Join your first curve if you chose one. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if requested, trace an example sail's boundary in the air, showing that the mast supplies one edge. Do not require the child to redraw a sail that has a gap. Shared edges are the proposed learning focus; the numbered order is not a universal drawing rule.

**Research basis:** R1–R3 inform this approach.

**Creative choice:** choose different sail sizes, a high or shallow hull and calm or lively waves. An imagined destination may guide later additions.

**Exploration turn:** “Where could your boat be going? Add your own idea to the sea or sky, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** another day, invent a boat with one sail. Invite the child to decide what changes when one sail is removed.

## intermediate-space-rocket — A rocket for an imagined journey

**Objective:** organise a central body, paired fins and nested details; distinguish large outlines from smaller interior shapes.

**Preparation turn:** “Turn your paper upright if you would like a tall rocket.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our rocket. Which parts are inside its body, and which stick out?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one tall rocket outline with a rounded point at the top. | Curve round the top and leave the base wider. |
| 2 | Draw one curve across the body near the top for the nose section. | Let the curve meet both sides. |
| 3 | Draw one round window inside the body. | Leave space around the window. |
| 4 | Draw one smaller round outline inside the window for its rim. | A gap between the two outlines can show the rim. |
| 5 | Add one pointed fin outside the lower left body. | A small triangular loop can meet the body. |
| 6 | Add one pointed fin outside the lower right body. | The fins can be different shapes. |
| 7 | Draw one shallow curve across the body near its base. | This marks a small engine section. |
| 8 | Draw one flame outline below the base. | Start at one side of the base and curl back to the other. |
| 9 | Draw one smaller flame curve inside it. | Let this curve echo part of the outer flame. |
| 10 | Draw one long stripe line down the left side of the body. | Keep the line inside the rocket. |
| 11 | Draw one long stripe line down the right side of the body. | Leave the window between the lines. |
| 12 | **Optional:** add one little button shape below the window. | A small circle or square can work. |
| 13 | **Optional:** if you added a button, add one more button, or skip. | If you choose another button, it can be a different shape. |
| 14 | **Optional:** draw one planet outline beside the rocket. | Choose a space outside the rocket's body. |
| 15 | **Optional:** if you added a planet, draw one ring curve round it, or skip. | A wide bend can cross the planet's middle if you chose one. |
| 16 | **Optional:** put one star dot in an empty part of the sky. | A pencil dot can stand for a distant star. |
| 17 | **Optional:** if you added a star dot, put one more in the sky, or skip. | Choose another empty space if you want another star. |
| 18 | **Optional:** draw one crescent-shaped moon outline. | A thin, curved loop can suggest a crescent. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if help is requested, compare the example's large body with its small window. Offer a larger window or fewer details as alternatives. Treat the journey as make-believe and do not present the stylised drawing as an engineering diagram.

**Research basis:** R1–R4 inform this combination of structure and invention.

**Creative choice:** choose the body proportions, fin shapes, window shape and an invented destination. Details need not be symmetrical.

**Exploration turn:** “What would your rocket find on its journey? Invent something on paper, keep drawing, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** try a submarine another day, using a large body and small windows but changing its direction and the parts that stick out.

## intermediate-house-and-garden — An imaginary house with a flower

**Objective:** connect simple building shapes and place a pathway and plant in relation to the house.

**Preparation turn:** “Leave some space around the middle of your paper for a garden.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our imaginary house. Where does the path begin?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one rectangle for the house. | Leave room above for a roof and below for a path. |
| 2 | Draw one sloping roof line from the top left corner towards a point above the middle. | Move upwards towards your chosen roof point. |
| 3 | Draw one sloping roof line from that point to the top right corner. | Let it meet the wall's corner. |
| 4 | Draw one door outline inside the lower part of the house. | Let the door meet the bottom of the wall. |
| 5 | Put one handle dot on the door. | Choose either side of the door. |
| 6 | Draw one window shape to the left of the door. | Leave a little wall around it. |
| 7 | Draw one window shape to the right of the door. | This window can be different. |
| 8 | Draw one line down the middle of the left window. | Keep it within the window outline. |
| 9 | Draw one line down the middle of the right window. | A slightly leaning line is welcome. |
| 10 | **Optional:** add one narrow chimney outline on the roof. | Let the little outline touch a roof slope. |
| 11 | Draw one path edge curving out from the door's left side. | Move down into the open garden space. |
| 12 | Draw one path edge curving out from the door's right side. | Leave space between the two edges. |
| 13 | Draw one flower stem beside the path. | Leave space above the stem for the flower. |
| 14 | Draw one little flower centre at the top of the stem. | A small round loop can work. |
| 15 | Add one petal curve above the flower centre. | Loop out from the centre and back. |
| 16 | Add one petal curve to the left of the centre. | This petal can be a different size. |
| 17 | Add one petal curve below the centre. | Leave the stem visible if you like. |
| 18 | Add one petal curve to the right of the centre. | A soft loop can finish this side. |
| 19 | Add one leaf outline beside the stem. | Let the little loop touch the stem. |
| 20 | **Optional:** draw one gentle ground curve beside the house. | Keep it separate from the path if you like. |
| 21 | **Optional:** draw one little stone outline beside the path. | An uneven oval can be a stone. |
| 22 | **Optional:** add one cloud outline above the house. | Choose an empty piece of sky. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** use an imaginary house; there is no need to identify the family's actual home. If the flower feels fiddly, offer a larger flower or a single round bloom. Discuss the path's attachment to the door only if requested. Do not require ruler-straight walls.

**Research basis:** R1–R4 inform the relational prompts and alternatives.

**Creative choice:** the child chooses window shapes, the path's direction and the flower's size or petal style. A house can become a tree-house or another invented home during exploration.

**Exploration turn:** “Who or what could enjoy this imaginary garden? Add your own idea, keep drawing, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** invent a little shop or a shed on another day. Invite the child to reuse walls, an opening and a roof while choosing a different arrangement.

**Exploration turn:** “What could your toy engine carry? Add a carriage, invent another idea, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** try a toy bus another day. Reuse wheels and a long body, then decide which locomotive parts to leave out.

## intermediate-potted-cactus — A cactus with reaching arms

**Objective:** attach branching outlines to a central stem; place a plant inside a container and add sparse surface marks.

**Preparation turn:** “Turn your paper upright if you would like a tall cactus.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our cactus. Where do its arms join the main stem?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one flattened oval for the pot's rim near the lower part of the paper. | Leave space underneath for the pot. |
| 2 | Draw one deep U-shaped outline beneath the rim for the pot. | Join the rim's left and right ends. |
| 3 | Draw one tall, rounded stem outline rising from the rim. | Go up one side, round the top and down the other. |
| 4 | Draw one bent line out from the stem's left side and upwards. | Let the end point up like a reaching arm. |
| 5 | Draw one returning bend from that arm's tip to the stem. | Follow beside the first bend with some space between. |
| 6 | Draw one bent line out from the stem's right side and upwards. | Choose how high this arm reaches. |
| 7 | Draw one returning bend from that arm's tip to the stem. | Let the bend meet the main stem. |
| 8 | Draw one short spine line on the stem's left side. | A tiny line can stick out from the edge. |
| 9 | Draw one short spine line on the stem's right side. | Choose a different spot. |
| 10 | Draw one more short spine line higher on the left side. | Leave space between the little lines. |
| 11 | Draw one more short spine line higher on the right side. | It can lean a different way. |
| 12 | Draw one long, gentle texture curve inside the stem. | Keep the curve between its outer edges. |
| 13 | Draw one decoration band across the pot. | Let the line follow the pot's rounded shape. |
| 14 | **Optional:** draw one more decoration band across the pot. | Leave a gap beside the first band. |
| 15 | **Optional:** draw one little flower centre near the top of the cactus. | A small circle can be the centre. |
| 16 | **Optional:** if you added a flower centre, add one petal loop on its left, or skip. | Loop out from the centre and back if you chose one. |
| 17 | **Optional:** if you added a flower centre, add one petal loop on its right, or skip. | You can choose this petal's size even if you skipped the left petal. |
| 18 | **Optional:** draw one little stone outline beside the pot. | A lumpy oval can suggest a stone. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** point to the example's arm connection only when requested. The outline of the main stem may remain visible through an arm attachment; erasing is unnecessary. Offer one arm or a simpler round cactus as a valid alternative.

**Research basis:** R1–R4 inform support without enforcing realism.

**Creative choice:** choose arm heights, an unbranched cactus, pot proportions or a decoration of the child's invention. A flower is optional.

**Exploration turn:** “Would you like another plant beside your cactus, a different pot pattern, or to finish here?”

**Later transfer invitation:** another day, invent a branching tree. Explore how one central form can support branches while changing the plant's outline and surface marks.

## intermediate-friendly-robot — A robot with a job you invent

**Objective:** connect separate forms with short joining lines and organise small features inside a head and control panel.

**Preparation turn:** “Leave some paper above and below the middle for your robot's head and feet.”

**Observation turn:** “Look at our robot. Which small lines join its bigger shapes?”

| Draw action | Exact child-facing instruction | Optional hint, on request |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Draw one rectangle for the robot's body. | Leave room for a head above it. |
| 2 | Draw one head outline above the body with a small gap between. | Try a square or a round outline. |
| 3 | Draw one short neck line across the left side of the gap. | Join the head to the body. |
| 4 | Draw one short neck line across the right side of the gap. | Leave space between the neck lines. |
| 5 | Draw one eye shape inside the head's left side. | A round loop or a dot can work. |
| 6 | Draw one eye shape inside the head's right side. | It can be different from the first. |
| 7 | Draw one mouth line below the eyes. | Choose a straight line or a gentle bend. |
| 8 | Draw one short antenna line above the head. | Start at the head's outline. |
| 9 | **Optional:** put one round shape at the antenna's tip. | A small loop can touch the line's end. |
| 10 | Draw one arm line out from the body's left side. | Choose where the arm points. |
| 11 | Draw one arm line out from the body's right side. | This arm can point another way. |
| 12 | Draw one hand shape at the end of the left arm. | A round loop can be a simple hand. |
| 13 | Draw one hand shape at the end of the right arm. | Choose another loop or a different outline. |
| 14 | Draw one leg line down from the body's lower left side. | Leave space underneath for a foot. |
| 15 | Draw one leg line down from the body's lower right side. | Keep a space between the legs. |
| 16 | Add one short foot line at the bottom of the left leg. | Point it outwards if you like. |
| 17 | Add one short foot line at the bottom of the right leg. | Your feet need not match. |
| 18 | Draw one panel outline inside the body. | Leave some body space around it. |
| 19 | Put one button dot inside the panel. | Choose any spot inside the outline. |
| 20 | **Optional:** put one more button dot inside the panel. | Leave a little space beside the first. |

**Parent scaffolding note:** if a joining line is difficult, invite the child to look at the corresponding gap in the example or to let the shapes touch instead. Treat the robot as an invented picture, not a real companion with authority or feelings. The child decides its fictional purpose.

**Research basis:** R1–R4 inform this guidance and creative agency.

**Creative choice:** choose the head shape, arm directions, hand shapes and mouth line. During exploration, invent a useful or silly job that changes what the robot carries or how it moves.

**Exploration turn:** “What job could your imaginary robot do? Add something for that job, keep drawing your own ideas, or finish here.”

**Later transfer invitation:** invent a different machine another day. Reuse connected forms and a panel, while choosing a new body shape and a different way to move.

## Review before implementation

An educator should check language, practical drawing demands and whether each action demonstrates the stated relationship. A visual reviewer should inspect every cumulative intermediate stage, including versions with optional details omitted. Parent-supervised formative sessions should check whether the prompts are understood and whether help leaves the child in control. In particular, inspect the turtle's shell divisions, the cactus arm joins and the locomotive's connected parts; these are likely places where a simpler alternative may be needed.

Do not equate completing these sequences with independent learning. Later transfer invitations are opportunities to explore without the same example, not scored assessments. Test the proposed curriculum before making claims about its effectiveness.
