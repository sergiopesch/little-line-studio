# Pacing, practice, and drawing medium: evidence notes

Researched 25 October 2026. These are bounded findings, not a systematic review or proof that the proposed app works.

## Learner-controlled pacing

**Hasler, Kersten & Sweller (2007), Learner control, cognitive load and instructional animation.** Full text inspected, including methods. Random assignment of 72 boys aged 9–11 to four formats for learning day/night science: continuous animation, discrete learner-paced segments, stop/play animation, narration alone. Learner-paced formats improved the more complex test questions; differences were not found for simpler questions. The reported instructional-efficiency advantage combines performance and subjective load; do not report an independently significant reduction in load without inspecting its test. Scope: older primary children, one science topic, immediate outcomes, one boys' school. It does not establish an optimal drawing pause, instruction word count, or benefit for preschoolers.

Source: [original article PDF](https://www.runi.ac.il/media/j2jbwbjw/learnercontrolcognitiveloadandinstructional.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1345).

**Design inference:** author meaningful stopping points; leave a still demonstration available; let the family explicitly continue. These are proposed adaptations, not experimentally validated app features.

## Practice quantity versus retention

**Ghanamah, Eghbaria-Ghanamah, Karni & Adi-Japha (2020), Too little, too much.** Publisher abstract and methods snippets accessible; full-text retrieval failed. Seventy-six children aged 7–8 across single-session 6/12/24-block and four-day 6-block schedules. A repeated invented-letter dot-connection task showed different patterns for immediate gains and retained performance: the most intensive single session did not yield the best retention; intermediate or distributed practice was advantageous. Narrow graphomotor task, not imaginative drawing. Do not convert experimental blocks into a prescribed number of minutes for children aged 3–10.

Source: [publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475219302701), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2020.101351).

**Design inference:** revisit a movement in different pictures on later days, with family choice and breaks. Never use streak pressure or relentless repetition. Independently test retention and transfer.

## Digital medium is part of the task

**Kirkorian et al. (2020; online 2019), Drawing across media.** Primary full text surfaced in search; direct PMC access later encountered CAPTCHA. Within-child comparison of 73 children aged 2–5 copying shapes with marker/paper, stylus/tablet, and finger/tablet; 24 adults were a comparison sample. Finger/tablet helped younger children produce codable shapes. This concerns immediate production, not long-term teaching efficacy, and does not imply all children should use fingers. Convenience sample and simple copying tasks constrain generalisation.

Source: [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31697094/), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7069367/).

**Design inference:** test touch, stylus, and mouse separately; offer large work areas and marks; do not score motor precision against the same standard across devices. Paper transfer requires its own assessment.

## Emerging research warrants extra scrutiny

**Ghanamah, Adi-Japha & Leukel (2026), Beyond repetition.** Open full text inspected. Eighty right-handed 7–8-year-olds, four groups of 20, randomized within classes to prompts/questions/both/neither during an invented-symbol task, followed through 5–6 weeks. Results favour prompts, especially for speed. However, school/class clustering was not modeled, some at-risk children were excluded, and instructions permit alternatives that scoring subsequently penalizes. The combined-condition accuracy advantage is weaker than the abstract's broad wording suggests. Novel graphomotor task, not artistic drawing; replication and transfer tests needed.

Source: [original open-access article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-026-09741-y).

**Design inference:** occasional optional strategy questions are worth testing for older children, not constant interrogation. Do not make this single recent study a cornerstone or advertise its task-specific effects as app benefits.
