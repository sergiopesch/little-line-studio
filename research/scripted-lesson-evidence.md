# Scripted, automatically paced paper lessons: evidence and design

Research update: 26 October 2026. Read alongside the existing [drawing](drawing-instruction-evidence.md), [pacing](pacing-practice-medium-evidence.md), [feedback](feedback-parent-evidence.md), and [music](background-music-evidence.md) notes. This scoped narrative update revisited original papers, publisher abstracts and author-institution records. It is not a systematic review or direct evaluation of DrawingTeacher.

## Recommendation

Offer a parent-started, automatically progressing lesson made from short, meaningful teaching segments, each followed by practice matched to the action. Keep a still reference and prominent **Pause / More time / Replay / Finish** controls. More time should suspend progression indefinitely until the family chooses to continue; no extra interaction is required when the normal pace suits them. Practice means a break in automatic teacher speech; optional music may continue.

This is a design inference intended to reduce repeated parent button presses while preserving control. The user's new request supersedes the earlier per-mark Next requirement in older notes; those notes do not prove that every mark needs a click. Neither automatic nor learner-paced delivery is universally superior. No reviewed study establishes optimal paper-drawing durations, validates this complete protocol, or demonstrates educational superiority of ElevenLabs voice or music.

The latest family feedback asks for less waiting. Standard outline drawing windows now begin at 12 seconds for Basic, 18 for Intermediate and 24 for Expert, adding two practice seconds per authored demonstration second beyond eight. Complete teaching packs override these defaults with per-action windows. Cat and Sun each contain 62 seconds of practice after teaching; their default estimates are 293.430 and 262.895 seconds. Native crayon demonstrations and actual media-completion gates remain intact. These revisions respond to observed interest, not a study-established optimal pace.

## Direct drawing evidence

**Vlach & Carver (2008): observation coaching.** Twenty-two kindergarten children were randomly assigned to brief observation coaching or drawing practice. Coaching supported greater improvements in detail and conceptual accuracy. Small, selected school sample; researcher-created scoring and limited independent rating; durable general transfer unestablished. Application: introduce one useful noticing cue, periodically reconnect a demonstrated mark to its subject, and leave room for the child's interpretation. This does not require a spoken quiz after every action. [Original full paper](https://ecrp.illinois.edu/v10n1/vlach.html).

**Bremner et al. (2000): copying, tracing and transfer.** Four experiments with children aged 7–10 distinguished improved cube copies from later drawings of a solid cube. Enforced stroke order improved copying without corresponding transfer; emphasis on meaningful faces supported transfer. Abstract-level access limits independent statistical assessment. Application: explain a relationship such as where a stem joins a flower; do not treat completing the prescribed sequence as independent learning. [Original PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10953929/), [publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00171).

## Adjacent pacing evidence: relevant, not drawing trials

**Hasler, Kersten & Sweller (2007).** Seventy-two boys aged 9–11 learned day/night science through continuous animation, learner-paced segments, stop/play animation, or narration alone. Learner-controlled conditions improved difficult-question performance, not simple-question performance. Stop/play was rarely used. Earlier full-text inspection in this repository found that subjective-load differences alone were not significant; the present refresh accessed the publisher abstract, while the institutional PDF timed out. Application: retain easy control and meaningful boundaries. This study does not prove either compulsory clicking or automatic timing for younger paper drawers. [Publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.1345).

**Spanjers et al. (2012).** Original full text checked. Secondary-school pupils were randomised to probability animations with/without pauses and temporal cues. Analysis included 161 after excluding ten who showed no/negative learning, a consequential outcome-based exclusion. The animations continued automatically after two-second pauses; users had no controls. Pausing improved immediate posttest scores modestly (6.46 versus 5.80 out of 8; partial eta-squared .03), without a significant pausing effect on reported mental effort. Application: built-in pauses can help in some contexts without clicks. These adolescents watched mathematical explanations; two seconds is **not** a validated interval for children to draw. No broad retention conclusion follows. [University-hosted original report](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/72615120/spanjers_2012_explaining_the_segmentations_effect.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.12.024).

**Biard, Cojean & Jamet (2018).** Publisher abstract and introductory material accessed; subsequent direct full-page opening was blocked. Sixty-eight occupational-therapy students studied a clinical procedure with noninteractive video, learner-controlled video, or segmented interactive video. Segmentation improved procedural performance; recall differences were nonsignificant, and pause controls were little used. This supports authored boundaries plus control for novices, not automatic paper-drawing efficacy. Do not equate system-inserted interruptions with a proven hands-free lesson: segmentation and permission to continue are separate dimensions. [Original publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563217306829), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2017.12.002).

## Scaffolding and feedback

**Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976).** A single tutor supported 30 children aged 3–5 in block construction. The original report describes simplifying demands, marking relevant features and adjusting help. It explicitly presents descriptive observations rather than a hypothesis test. Application: a requested hint can simplify the current movement or show a larger variation; the exact help ladder is authored design. [Original report](https://w.pauldowling.me/tmr/readings/Wood,%20Bruner%20&%20Ross.pdf).

**Kamins & Dweck (1999).** Two kindergarten role-play experiments (N=67 and N=64) compared person-, outcome- and process-focused feedback. Person versus process feedback produced less adaptive immediate setback responses. These are not long-term art outcomes. Application: warm, useful strategy language rather than talent judgments or generic pressure. Since the app cannot see paper, avoid fabricated observations such as “Your circle is perfect” or “You worked really hard.” Prefer invitations: “A wobbly shape can be part of your idea.” [Original report](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/infancy/pdf/person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf).

## Background music: enjoyment option, not a learning treatment

**Sarı et al. (2019).** A randomised study of 99 children aged 4–6 compared digital-storybook visual and auditory enhancements. Music/background sounds did not improve comprehension and impaired receptive vocabulary. The auditory package bundled music and sounds, so instrumental music alone was not isolated. Institutional original abstract checked; complete statistical methods not audited. Application: protect spoken instructions from competing audio. [Author-institution record](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/a-randomized-controlled-trial-to-test-efficacy-of-digital-enhance/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2018.11.006).

**Hallam, Price & Katsarou (2002).** Original report checked. In 31 children aged 10–11, calming music improved arithmetic work rate without a significant accuracy benefit. A separate N=30 study with children aged 11–12 found better cued recall with pleasant/calming music and worse recall with arousing/unpleasant music. Small, short, recording-specific tasks, largely beyond this product's age range. These findings cannot establish a universal calming playlist or improved drawing. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/03055690220124551), [full report inspected](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2002-hallam.pdf).

Music lessons, music therapy, adult creativity experiments and listening during a drawing lesson are distinct interventions. A soundtrack sounding gentle does not establish attention, wellbeing or learning benefits. Keep music easy to mute and dispensable. Prefer restrained instrumental arrangements without words or salient sound effects; that is a conservative design choice, not a proven optimum. The earlier research recommendation favoured muting during instruction. The current user-requested policy instead defaults music on after the adult's Begin gesture and ducks it beneath speech, with a pre-start opt-out and immediate mute. This is a product preference rather than stronger scientific evidence. Test the soundtrack separately from pacing so one cannot hide harm from the other.

## Practical authoring and parent-relief design

1. **Parent start, then guided flow.** Explain once: “The lesson will move on gently. Pause or choose More time whenever you like.” Parent presence remains required; reducing taps is not removing supervision.
2. **One idea at a time.** A concise instruction identifies the next action and meaningful location. Demonstrate it at a readable speed; keep previous marks and the current still state visible.
3. **Separate teaching time from practice time.** After narration and demonstration, provide a quiet, per-action drawing window. Time for a dot differs from time for a complex outline. Do not advance solely because a voice clip or animation ended. Avoid speech that demands simultaneous watching and precise drawing.
4. **Make timing adjustable, without diagnostic claims.** Offer a slower overall pace and unlimited More time. Start from the authored action, then revise from observed comprehension, interest, requests and comfort. Avoid padding gaps with unrelated speech or retaining waits solely to meet a total session length. No evidence establishes exact seconds, words per minute, age-based pace presets or a fixed session length here.
5. **Preserve control without constant questions.** Pause freezes audio, animation and progression. Replay returns to the current action and supplies fresh drawing time. Resume does not race to catch up. Never infer readiness from silence or inactivity. Hidden tabs and interrupted audio should pause rather than silently skip instruction.
6. **Offer details as genuine invitations.** Clearly say that an optional detail can be left out while the guided lesson continues. Dependency wording must remain understandable if earlier optional marks were omitted. Do not announce that an unseen detail is complete.
7. **Leave creative space.** End the guided sequence with an open invitation and unlimited quiet exploration, not an automatic new lesson or a compulsory polished result. Revisit a related new subject another day without requiring the same model.
8. **Keep the teaching script authoritative, not the voice model.** ElevenLabs can produce the reviewed voice/music assets; provider choice does not determine pedagogy. Review pronunciation, warmth, intelligibility, levels and sudden transitions. Pre-generated, cached assets let the deterministic lesson controller own transitions. No model permission to change settings, authorize actions, capture speech or evaluate unseen drawings is needed.

## What to validate before efficacy claims

Compare the automatic segmented experience with an equally supported learner-paced option on the same subjects. Measure parent control workload, child comprehension and comfort, unwanted advance events, pause/replay requests, later independent application and retention separately. Check access needs and different drawing speeds; a pooled completion rate can hide children being left behind. Pilot family observations can improve scripts and timings but cannot establish learning efficacy. Keep supervision and safeguards fixed across comparisons, and assess whether the soundtrack adds enjoyment without impairing instruction. No study above validates a universally best method, exact timing rule, commercial voice, or complete app.
